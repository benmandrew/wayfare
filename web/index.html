Wayfare

Services by road, and by route where there is no road

Most lines are a road segment — a stretch of road between two junctions. Thickness shows how many distinct bus and coach services use it, so trunk corridors stand out from the tail.

Colour shows journeys a day — the departures along that road, averaged over the whole week and drawn on a logarithmic scale. Thickness and colour answer different questions, so a lane with one service every ten minutes is thin and bright. Grey means the archive under you carries no departure count at that zoom.

Trams, metros, trains and ferries have no road under them, so they are drawn from the operator's own trace and keep a colour of their own, with a bar each in the key below. Every bar is the same scale, so a quiet ferry and a busy one are the same red at different depths. Each line is one route's trace, so where two share a track they are two lines, not one busier one.

National Rail is drawn from OpenStreetMap route relations, because Great Britain's rail timetable is not in the open bus feed. It is one line per stretch of track rather than one per service, and it is a single flat brown rather than a bar: no timetable is attributed to it, so nothing here knows how often a train runs. Hovering still lists which services use that stretch.

Each row of the key is a switch. Clear its checkbox to take that mode off the map.

Hover a line for its service list. Filtering matches whole service numbers, so 4 will not match 42.

Zoomed out, the map thins the densest areas to keep tiles small, so a quiet-looking city is not necessarily a city without services. Zoom in for the complete picture.
